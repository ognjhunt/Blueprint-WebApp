# Blueprint's commercial model

Free discovery → prepaid evaluation credit → activation → recurring robot-months.

This replaces the site-paid 5% success fee on collected provider revenue. The
old model is removed from the codebase rather than left alongside this one, so
the product states a single thing.

## Why not a percentage of the robot contract

Three reasons, in order of severity.

1. **Neither party can verify it.** A percentage of a confidential contract is
   disputable, needs audit rights to collect, and gives both sides a shared
   reason to route around Blueprint. An activated site-task and an active
   robot-month are countable by either party from the deployment record.
2. **It puts all revenue behind an event Blueprint does not control**, months
   after Blueprint has already spent the capture cost. That is a startup
   financing the industry's discovery costs on speculation.
3. **A separate surcharge creates a shared saving.** If the fee is a line item
   the site pays on top, both parties profit from Blueprint's absence.

A 1–2% first-year rate is retained as an alternative, offered only where
Blueprint controls invoicing or receives audited reporting — the cases where
the number is observable. Renewals price substantially lower.

## The four rules

1. **Subsidise what costs nothing at the margin and adds liquidity.** Anonymous
   listings, automated fit screening, one standardised evaluation, and site
   qualification. These run against a record Blueprint already holds; rationing
   them would slow the market more than it would earn.
2. **Charge before someone consumes scarce manual work, site access, or
   identifiable opportunity information.** Named-site detail is the line.
3. **Tie recurring revenue to an observable unit.**
4. **Make the recurring fee buy something ongoing.** If Blueprint's
   contribution ends at the introduction, bypass is rational and no contract
   language fixes it.

## The schedule

| Stage | Charge | Unit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Discovery, screening, one standard eval, site qualification | $0 | — |
| Evaluation credit | $2,500–$5,000 | per site-task, per team |
| Deployment activation | $5,000 | per activated site-task |
| Active robot-month | $100–$300 | per robot, per month |

**The credit is returned in full** against activation and the first year of
robot-months. A team that deploys therefore pays nothing extra for having
evaluated; a team that consumes named-site access and walks away funds the
capture pipeline it consumed. This is what keeps the fee from taxing the
outcome we want.

Billed separately: reserved or extended compute, model adaptation and custom
engineering, data rights beyond the single-eval licence.

## Nothing expensive on expressed interest

A capture visit spends an operator relationship and a slot on a working floor.
One of these must hold first: a verified project budget, a signed pilot-intent
document, or a refundable commitment.

## What the recurring fee buys

Maintained testbed, acceptance-test versioning, re-evaluation when the robot or
workcell changes, an independent deployment record, expansion evaluation, and
incident evidence. Both parties also sign an opportunity-specific
acknowledgment covering attribution, reporting and audit before identifiable
detail is released — the backstop, not the defence.

## What Blueprint does not do

Robot teams keep deployment and the data loop. Agility's own published process
has its engineers recreate the workcell, generate task data, install, configure
and tune on site; Agility says deployments generate proprietary data, Figure
says its BMW deployment informed Figure 03's hardware, and Apptronik supplies
real-world deployment data into its Google DeepMind partnership. That is where
their advantage compounds and it is not for sale.

Blueprint removes the work in front of it: site discovery, qualification,
evidence-gathering, and handoff. Note that "robot teams never outsource
deployment" would be too strong — Bessemer's own follow-up says integration
partnerships are becoming increasingly viable. The position is about which part
of the work, not about outsourcing in general.

## On the numbers

**These are posted starting terms Blueprint intends to test, not market rates,
and no surface may present them as an industry benchmark.**

No independent source establishes a market price for this service. The two
figures usually cited do not support one:

- Robotics-data benchmarks ($50k–$200k dataset budgets, per-hour teleoperation
  rates) are published largely by vendors selling data collection services.
  They show robotics data can be expensive; they are not market-clearing
  prices.
- The customer-paid deployment fee of roughly $20,000–$25,000 comes from
  Agility's June 2026 investor materials and is explicitly an illustrative
  figure inside one company's own model, not an industry standard.

Public pilot announcements generally do not disclose who paid. "The robot
company paid to deploy" usually means it absorbed robot time, engineering,
travel, integration and a discounted pilot — not that the facility invoiced it
for access. There is no reliable rule that early teams pay and mature teams get
paid.

## On scarcity

Qualified, budgeted sites are valuable, but so are commercially capable robots
and deployment engineers. As of August 2026 humanoids remain early-stage with
limited commercial demand, unresolved reliability and cost problems, and — in
China particularly — manufacturing capacity ahead of genuine demand. This is
**bilateral scarcity**, not "sites are scarce, therefore never charge sites."
The model should stay adjustable on which side carries more of the fee.

## On positioning

Blueprint is **deployment qualification and acceptance infrastructure**, not an
underwriter. An underwriter takes or prices risk. The underwriter framing only
becomes accurate if customers contractually rely on Blueprint's certification
or Blueprint assumes some performance risk — neither is true today.

## Leakage, stated accurately

Blueprint has the structural characteristics that make disintermediation
dangerous: high-value contracts, prolonged in-person interaction, custom
negotiation, and repeat relationships. That much is real.

The commonly cited marketplace-leakage statistics are not. The "18%" figure
found in the literature is the *reduction* in disintermediation observed when
one freelance marketplace blocked Skype — not a claim that 18% of marketplace
transactions leak. No strong evidence supports the 30–80% range that circulates
informally. The design here does not rest on those numbers; it rests on
collecting most revenue before the parties are alone together, and on the
recurring fee buying something.
